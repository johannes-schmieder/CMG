Combinatorial Multigrid, Step by Step
A visual and numerical guide to graph-Laplacian solving

CMG project teaching supplement

28 August 2026

Abstract

Many large estimation, network, imaging, and simulation problems eventually ask the same numerical question: solve a sparse graph-Laplacian system. Conjugate gradients (CG) avoids forming a dense inverse, but can still make slow progress when the graph contains weak bridges or widely separated scales. Preconditioned conjugate gradients (PCG) improves the geometry of the problem. Combinatorial Multigrid (CMG) supplies the preconditioner by grouping strongly connected vertices, solving related problems on progressively smaller graphs, and lifting the coarse correction back to the original graph.

This supplement develops that sequence from first principles. A weighted 12-vertex graph makes every hierarchy level and several matrix blocks visible. A second example is computed from a public Veneto worker–firm teaching extract: it shows an anonymized real graph neighborhood, actual entries of its Laplacian, a materialized block of the CMG action, the complete hierarchy sizes, and certified solve diagnostics. Every numerical exhibit is generated by the production Rust implementation in this repository.

1 The problem hiding inside a graph

1.1 A network becomes a matrix

Let a graph have vertices 1,…,n1,\ldots,n. An undirected edge {i,j}\{i,j\} carries a positive weight wijw_{ij}. A large weight means that vertices ii and jj are strongly coupled. The weighted degree of vertex ii is di=∑j:{i,j}∈Ewij.d_i=\sum_{j:\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}. The graph Laplacian is the symmetric matrix L∈ℝn×nL\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} with Lii=di,Lij=−wij(i≠j),L_{ii}=d_i, \qquad L_{ij}=-w_{ij}\quad(i\neq j), where Lij=0L_{ij}=0 when there is no edge. Each edge therefore contributes the small block wij[1−1−11]w_{ij} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1\\[-0.2em] -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} to rows and columns ii and jj.

For the weighted path 1−22−131\mathrel{-^{2}}2\mathrel{-^{1}}3, for example, L=[2−20−23−10−11].L= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 & 0\\ -2 & 3 & -1\\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. The diagonal records the total incident weight, while each negative off-diagonal records one link. Every row sums to zero.

1.2 Three equivalent ways to read Lx=bLx=b

The equation Lx=bLx=b can be read in several useful ways.

  1. Balance. The iith equation is (Lx)i=∑jwij(xi−xj)=bi.(Lx)_i=\sum_j w_{ij}(x_i-x_j)=b_i. Each neighboring difference contributes a weighted flow, and the flows balance the external quantity bib_i.

  2. Energy. For every vector xx, x⊤Lx=∑{i,j}∈Ewij(xi−xj)2.x^\top Lx=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} w_{ij}(x_i-x_j)^2. \tag{1}\label{eq:energy} The energy is small when strongly linked vertices receive similar values.

  3. Optimization. A solution minimizes ϕ(x)=12x⊤Lx−b⊤x,\phi(x)=\tfrac12 x^\top Lx-b^\top x, \tag{2}\label{eq:quadratic} because ∇ϕ(x)=Lx−b\nabla\phi(x)=Lx-b.

The energy identity also proves that LL is positive semidefinite: its quadratic form cannot be negative. That property is exactly what CG needs, after we handle the null space.

1.3 Why the Laplacian is singular

If 𝟏\mathbf{1} is the all-ones vector, then L𝟏=0L\mathbf{1}=0. Adding the same constant to all entries of xx leaves every difference xi−xjx_i-x_j unchanged. On a connected graph, Lx=b⇒L(x+c𝟏)=bLx=b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L(x+c\mathbf{1})=b for every scalar cc. There is a solution only when 𝟏⊤b=0.\mathbf{1}^\top b=0. \tag{3}\label{eq:compatibility} For a disconnected graph, the sum must be zero separately within each connected component. The Rust solver checks this compatibility condition and removes only accepted floating-point drift. It reports a deterministic component-wise normalization so repeated runs agree.

Key idea.  The singularity is not a numerical accident. It expresses a real invariance: only differences are identified. We solve on the quotient space obtained after ignoring component-wise constants.

1.4 Why not compute L−1L^{-1}?

First, a Laplacian has no ordinary inverse. Even after grounding one vertex or imposing a zero-mean restriction, the inverse is usually dense. Storing a dense n×nn\times n matrix costs O(n2)O(n^2) memory. By contrast, a sparse Laplacian stores roughly one diagonal entry per vertex and two matrix entries per edge, and a multiplication y=Lxy=Lx costs O(n+m)O(n+m) for mm edges.

The right strategy is therefore iterative: use only sparse matrix–vector products, vector updates, and a carefully chosen approximate inverse.

2 Conjugate gradients: solving without an inverse

2.1 Residuals are both errors in the equation and directions

Suppose the current guess is xkx_k. Its residual is rk=b−Lxk.r_k=b-Lx_k. Because ∇ϕ(xk)=Lxk−b=−rk\nabla\phi(x_k)=Lx_k-b=-r_k, the residual points in the direction of steepest decrease of the quadratic objective introduced above. Steepest descent would repeatedly move along rkr_k. On a stretched quadratic bowl it tends to zig-zag.

CG instead constructs search directions pkp_k that are mutually LL-conjugate: pi⊤Lpj=0(i≠j).p_i^\top Lp_j=0 \qquad (i\neq j). Moving along a new direction does not undo the minimization already achieved along earlier directions. This is the core idea of the method introduced by Hestenes and Stiefel (1952).

2.2 The CG recurrence

Starting from x0=0x_0=0, ordinary CG performs the following operations: r0=b,p0=r0,αk=rk⊤rkpk⊤Lpk,xk+1=xk+αkpk,rk+1=rk−αkLpk,βk=rk+1⊤rk+1rk⊤rk,pk+1=rk+1+βkpk.\begin{aligned} r_0&=b, & p_0&=r_0,\\ \alpha_k&=\frac{r_k^\top r_k}{p_k^\top Lp_k}, & x_{k+1}&=x_k+\alpha_kp_k,\\ r_{k+1}&=r_k-\alpha_kLp_k, & \beta_k&=\frac{r_{k+1}^\top r_{k+1}}{r_k^\top r_k},\\ p_{k+1}&=r_{k+1}+\beta_kp_k.&& \end{aligned} Only one multiplication by LL is needed per iteration. In exact arithmetic, CG solves an nn-dimensional positive definite problem within at most nn steps. In practice, rounding error and a large condition number can make many iterations necessary.

For a connected Laplacian, ignore the zero eigenvalue and write the remaining eigenvalues as 0<λ2≤⋯≤λn.0<\lambda_2\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_n. The quotient-space condition number is κ(L)=λn/λ2\kappa(L)=\lambda_n/\lambda_2. A small λ2\lambda_2 often signals a weak bridge: it is cheap in energy to move one side of the graph relative to the other. CG then has to resolve both very soft and very stiff directions.

Checkpoint.  If all edge weights are multiplied by ten, LL and every nonzero eigenvalue are multiplied by ten, but κ(L)\kappa(L) is unchanged. Conditioning is about relative scales, not the overall unit of measurement.

2.3 Stopping requires a numerical statement

A small recursive residual is not enough by itself: accumulated rounding may separate the maintained residual from b−Lxb-Lx. This crate periodically recomputes the original-system residual and always verifies a convergence candidate. It reports relative residual=‖b−Lx‖2‖b‖2\text{relative residual}=\frac{\lVert b-Lx\rVert_2}{\lVert b\rVert_2} and the scale-aware backward error η=‖b−Lx‖2‖b‖2+‖L‖bound‖x‖2.\eta=\frac{\lVert b-Lx\rVert_2} {\lVert b\rVert_2+\lVert L\rVert_{\mathrm{bound}}\lVert x\rVert_2}. \tag{4}\label{eq:backward} The latter asks: relative to the sizes of the submitted data and computed solution, how small a perturbation would make xx exact? A small backward error does not automatically imply a small forward error ‖x−x⋆‖\lVert x-x_\star\rVert when LL is ill-conditioned.

3 Preconditioned conjugate gradients

3.1 Change the geometry, not the answer

A preconditioner is an operator that is inexpensive to apply and behaves roughly like the inverse of LL. It is common to write a positive definite matrix M≈LM\approx L and apply M−1M^{-1}. In this supplement we write B≈L+,B\approx L^+, where BB denotes the action actually applied to a compatible residual and L+L^+ is the Laplacian pseudoinverse. The implementation never forms a dense BB; it evaluates z=Brz=Br through a multilevel cycle.

PCG replaces the raw residual direction with the preconditioned residual zk=Brk.z_k=Br_k. Its central recurrence is αk=rk⊤zkpk⊤Lpk,xk+1=xk+αkpk,rk+1=rk−αkLpk,zk+1=Brk+1,βk=rk+1⊤zk+1rk⊤zk,pk+1=zk+1+βkpk.\begin{aligned} \alpha_k&=\frac{r_k^\top z_k}{p_k^\top Lp_k}, &x_{k+1}&=x_k+\alpha_kp_k,\\ r_{k+1}&=r_k-\alpha_kLp_k, &z_{k+1}&=Br_{k+1},\\ \beta_k&=\frac{r_{k+1}^\top z_{k+1}}{r_k^\top z_k}, &p_{k+1}&=z_{k+1}+\beta_kp_k. \end{aligned} The target solution remains Lx=bLx=b. The preconditioner changes which error directions are easy for the iteration to remove.

An ideal B=L+B=L^+ would solve the quotient-space problem in one application. That would be as expensive as solving the original problem. A useful preconditioner is deliberately imperfect: applying it must be much cheaper than the iterations it saves.

4 How CMG constructs the approximate inverse

4.1 The multilevel principle

Ordinary relaxation quickly removes error that changes sharply across adjacent vertices. It is much slower on smooth error, such as nearly constant values on either side of a weak bridge. Multigrid represents this smooth error on a smaller graph, where the same large-scale pattern becomes a short-scale pattern that is easier to correct.

CMG makes the smaller graphs using combinatorial information—the weighted connections—rather than a geometric mesh. The hierarchy is deterministic in this Rust port.

4.2 Step 1: choose heavy incident connections

At a level ℓ\ell, let the graph be GℓG_\ell with Laplacian LℓL_\ell. Each non-isolated vertex selects its maximum-weight incident edge. Equal-weight ties choose the lower-numbered neighbor. These selections define a directed parent relation. Intuitively, a vertex first proposes to stay with the neighbor to which it is most strongly coupled.

The selected relation may contain long or poorly shaped components. The implementation applies the upstream diameter/conductance forest split, then a low-effective-degree correction. If the selected tree weight incident to a vertex is too small relative to that vertex’s weighted degree, the vertex becomes its own root. The upstream threshold used here is 1/81/8.

4.3 Step 2: turn forest components into aggregates

The connected components of the corrected forest are the aggregates. If fine vertex ii belongs to coarse aggregate aa, define the restriction matrix RℓR_\ell by (Rℓ)ai=1,(R_\ell)_{a i}=1, with all other entries zero. Restriction sums a fine vector within each aggregate: rc=Rℓrf.r_c=R_\ell r_f. Prolongation copies a coarse correction back to each member: ef=Rℓ⊤ec.e_f=R_\ell^\top e_c.

4.4 Step 3: contract the graph exactly

The next Laplacian is the Galerkin contraction Lℓ+1=RℓLℓRℓ⊤.L_{\ell+1}=R_\ell L_\ell R_\ell^\top. \tag{5}\label{eq:galerkin} Edges inside one aggregate disappear; weights of multiple edges between the same two aggregates are summed. This Galerkin contraction preserves the energy of vectors that are constant within each aggregate: y⊤Lℓ+1y=(Rℓ⊤y)⊤Lℓ(Rℓ⊤y).y^\top L_{\ell+1}y=(R_\ell^\top y)^\top L_\ell(R_\ell^\top y). The same construction repeats until a terminal condition is reached. Production CMG uses a direct grounded LDL factor when a level has fewer than 700 vertices. Fill growth, weak vertex reduction, full contraction, and a maximum-level guard provide safe alternative stopping conditions.

4.5 Step 4: apply one stationary CMG cycle

At a nonterminal level, the smoother uses D̃ℓ−1=diag(12di).\widetilde D_\ell^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{1}{2d_i}\right). Given a compatible right-hand side rr, one cycle has the following shape:

  1. form an initial damped-Jacobi approximation e←D̃ℓ−1re\leftarrow \widetilde D_\ell^{-1}r;

  2. compute the remaining residual s←r−Lℓes\leftarrow r-L_\ell e;

  3. restrict and center it, sc←Rℓss_c\leftarrow R_\ell s;

  4. approximately solve Lℓ+1ec=scL_{\ell+1}e_c=s_c recursively, using the stored repeat count;

  5. prolong and add, e←e+Rℓ⊤ece\leftarrow e+R_\ell^\top e_c;

  6. apply a final damped-Jacobi correction.

At a direct terminal, a grounded LDL factor solves the compatible coarse problem. Repeat counts balance recursive work using nonzero counts and, near a direct terminal, the actual terminal-factor fill.

Key idea.  Smoothing attacks local, rapidly varying error. Coarse correction attacks global, slowly varying error. CMG alternates the two, and PCG corrects whatever the stationary cycle does not remove accurately enough.

5 A 12-vertex example, all the way down

5.1 The graph and its pedagogical setting

The teaching graph has 12 vertices and 16 weighted edges. Vertices V1–V6 form one well-connected lobe, V7–V12 form another, and two weak cross-links of weights 0.40.4 and 0.250.25 connect the lobes. Within each lobe, the strongest edge has weight 44, followed by edges of weights 33, 22, and smaller links.

The production direct threshold is 700, so a 12-vertex graph would normally go directly to the terminal factor. To expose the hierarchy, the teaching driver alone sets direct_threshold = 3. No library default or production path is changed.

At level 0, CMG forms four aggregates: {V1,V2,V3,V4},{V5,V6},{V7,V8,V9,V10},{V11,V12}.\{\mathrm{V1,V2,V3,V4}\},\quad \{\mathrm{V5,V6}\},\quad \{\mathrm{V7,V8,V9,V10}\},\quad \{\mathrm{V11,V12}\}. 5.3 A complete solve

The driver chooses a deterministic zero-mean vector x⋆x_\star, constructs b=Lx⋆b=Lx_\star, builds CMG, and runs the production certified PCG solver with its default relative backward tolerance of 10−810^{-8}. It completes in 7 iterations. The freshly recomputed relative residual is 6.072 × 10−9, the backward error is 8.414 × 10−10, and the maximum coordinate difference from the known zero-mean x⋆x_\star is 6.988 × 10−9.

6 A real worker–firm graph from the Veneto teaching extract

6.1 What the public data are—and are not

The second example uses inst/extdata/test.csv from version 0.1.0 of the public CRAN package LeaveOutKSS (Moghani 2026). Its SHA-256 digest is

93e57a413a8cfccdcb043c5d793105a67b2dc9ebd27d5d3a4f1800abf89a2241.

It is a public teaching extract associated with a Veneto worker–firm application. It is not the full confidential Veneto Workers History data. The build checks the digest, keeps source identifiers only in memory, and writes no source worker ID, firm ID, or outcome to the repository. Displayed nodes receive fresh labels W1, W2, … and F1, F2, ….

6.2 Why worker–firm normal equations become a Laplacian

In the two-way fixed-effects model of Abowd, Kramarz, and Margolis (1999), a simplified observation equation is yit=αi+ψJ(i,t)+εit.y_{it}=\alpha_i+\psi_{J(i,t)}+\varepsilon_{it}. Let DWD_W select the worker and DFD_F select the firm for each row. Flip the sign convention of the firm effect and define the incidence matrix C=[DW,−DF].C=[D_W,-D_F]. Then L=C⊤CL=C^\top C is the Laplacian of a bipartite worker–firm graph. A worker–firm pair observed ww times contributes an edge of weight ww. Worker and firm diagonals count their weighted observations, while the corresponding off-diagonal is −w-w.

This construction isolates the numerical graph problem. It does not by itself partial out controls, estimate a complete AKM model, prune leave-out connected sets, or calculate the variance corrections studied by Kline, Saggio, and Sølvsten (2020). Those tasks are statistical layers around the solver.

6.3 The graph used here

The public file has 71614 rows, 35807 distinct workers, 5301 distinct firms, and 44050 distinct worker–firm pairs. The complete bipartite graph has 1900 connected components. For a clean single-component numerical illustration, the driver uses the largest component: 34862 vertices=31731 workers+3131 firms,34862\text{ vertices} =31731\text{ workers} +3131\text{ firms}, with 39652 canonical weighted edges.

6.7 A certified solve and what its diagnostics mean

The driver constructs a deterministic zero-mean truth x⋆x_\star, forms b=Lx⋆b=Lx_\star, and solves with the production defaults. PCG completes in 16 iterations. Its recomputed relative residual is 1.840 × 10−6, while the certified backward error is 9.322 × 10−9. The two values differ because the stopping rule uses the scale-aware denominator shown in the stopping criterion; this is expected, and both are reported so the distinction is visible.

The maximum coordinate difference from the known normalized truth is 6.977 × 10−3. This is much larger than the backward error, illustrating a central numerical lesson: an ill-conditioned system can have a very accurate equation residual while some solution coordinates remain more sensitive. Statistical applications should choose tolerances with the downstream estimand in mind, not treat one residual number as a universal accuracy guarantee.

Key idea.  On this real graph, the hierarchy shrinks the problem by more than two orders of magnitude before the direct terminal. The resulting preconditioner is global even though the original matrix is sparse, and PCG still performs the final accuracy-controlled correction.

7 Putting the pieces together

For a new graph-Laplacian solve, the full sequence is:

  1. Assemble and validate. Canonicalize positive weighted edges, form the diagonal, identify components, and verify that bb is component-compatible.

  2. Build once. Construct the heavy-edge forest, forest splits, aggregates, Galerkin coarse graphs, smoothers, repeat counts, and terminal factor.

  3. Allocate reusable workspaces. Keep recursion and PCG vectors out of repeated allocation paths.

  4. Apply CMG inside PCG. Each zk=Brkz_k=Br_k uses the stored stationary cycle. The graph and hierarchy do not change between iterations.

  5. Certify. Recompute b−Lxb-Lx, report residual and backward error, and retain the component-wise normalization.

  6. Reuse when possible. If edge weights do not change, the same preconditioner and workspace can solve many compatible right-hand sides. Repeated solves are important in randomized projections, leverage calculations, and leave-out procedures.

8 Common misconceptions

“CMG returns the coarse solution.” No. Coarse solutions are corrections lifted back to the original graph. PCG returns a finest-level solution.

“The preconditioner is a dense stored inverse.” No. Dense blocks in this supplement are diagnostic materializations. Production CMG stores sparse graphs, aggregate maps, diagonal smoothers, a small terminal factor, and workspaces.

“A small residual means every coordinate is accurate.” Not necessarily. Backward accuracy and forward accuracy differ, especially for ill-conditioned graphs.

“More levels are always better.” No. Coarsening must save more work than recursion costs. CMG uses fill and stagnation guards as well as a direct threshold.

“Any symmetric matrix is a graph Laplacian.” No. A graph Laplacian has nonpositive off-diagonals, zero row sums, and nonnegative edge weights. The crate also supports SDDM systems through its documented augmentation, but not arbitrary indefinite or nonsymmetric matrices.

9 Exercises for checking understanding

  1. For the three-vertex example in Section 1, verify the energy identity by multiplying out x⊤Lxx^\top Lx.

  2. Explain why b=(1,0,0)⊤b=(1,0,0)^\top is incompatible on a connected three-vertex graph. Construct the closest vector obtained by subtracting its mean.

  3. In the toy hierarchy, write the 4×124\times12 restriction matrix R0R_0 from the four displayed aggregates. What does R0rR_0r do to a residual vector?

  4. Why can the displayed Veneto principal block have a diagonal of 7311 but only seven visible −1-1 entries in that row?

  5. Suppose an application solves 1,000 right-hand sides on an unchanged graph. Which costs can be amortized, and which operations must still occur for every right-hand side?

  6. A run has backward error 10−910^{-9} but a forward error of 10−310^{-3}. Give a graph feature that can make both statements true.
